Liberty, Property Rights, and the Conditions for Progress

Session 10 · Thu Oct 1

Why do prices and institutions matter for progress, and why is liberty never just a technical problem of efficient markets?

One town, two countries

Nogales is divided by a fence.

On the north side is Nogales, Arizona. On the south side is Nogales, Sonora. The climate is the same. The landscape is the same. Families, language, food, and everyday culture cross the border. People on either side are not separated by deep geography or ancient biology. They are separated by institutions.

Acemoglu and Robinson use Nogales to pose one of the most important questions in political economy: why are some places prosperous while others are not? Their answer is not that some peoples are more capable, or that geography alone determines destiny. Their answer is that political and economic rules shape incentives: who can own property, start firms, borrow money, enforce contracts, challenge power, and keep the fruits of their work.

Hayek asks a related question from another angle. Even if a society wants prosperity, how can it possibly know what to produce, where resources should go, and which plans are worth pursuing? His answer is prices. Prices communicate dispersed knowledge that no planner can gather in one place.

The question this session is about: what conditions make decentralized progress possible, and what happens when liberty is separated from power?

This is the “markets need institutions” session. Start with Nogales because it keeps the institutional argument concrete. Then use Hayek to explain why decentralized decision-making matters inside those institutions. The danger is students hearing either reading as a slogan. Keep pulling them back to mechanisms.

Prices as compressed knowledge

Hayek’s central claim is simple and radical: the knowledge needed to coordinate an economy is dispersed among millions of people.

A farmer knows local soil and weather. A machinist knows which tool is wearing out. A parent knows the timing constraints of a child’s school day. A shop owner knows which goods are not moving. A scientist knows which experiment failed last week. None of this knowledge can be fully written down, sent to a ministry, and optimized from the center.

Prices help solve this problem. A price is not merely a number. It is a signal that compresses information about scarcity, opportunity, cost, demand, and alternative uses. When the price of copper rises, millions of people do not need to know exactly which mine flooded or which technology increased demand. They only need to know that copper has become more valuable at the margin, and they can adjust.

Prices allow people to coordinate using knowledge they do not personally possess.

This is why Hayek thinks markets are epistemic institutions, not just incentive machines. They help societies use knowledge that exists only in fragments.

Hayek says prices communicate knowledge. What kinds of knowledge do prices communicate poorly or not at all? Think about pollution, care work, public health, or scientific research.

What is Hayek's main argument in "The Use of Knowledge in Society"?

  1. A central planner can coordinate an economy if it has enough data.
  2. Prices help coordinate dispersed knowledge that no single person or agency can collect.
  3. Only scientific experts have economically useful knowledge.
  4. Markets work because people ignore information from others.

Board move: draw two columns, “knowledge in one place” and “knowledge dispersed.” Ask students what kind of knowledge a central planner could know, and what kind would be lost. Then ask where prices help and where they fail.

Property rights and permission to act

Prices can only coordinate action if people are allowed to act on them.

That is why property rights matter. If you cannot own land securely, you will not invest in improving it. If contracts are not enforced, trade becomes risky. If officials can seize profits, entrepreneurs hide rather than expand. If entry into a profession or market depends on political connections, talent is wasted.

But property rights are not magic words. They are political arrangements. Someone decides whose property counts, whose contracts are enforced, whose harms are ignored, and who has standing to challenge abuse. A society can have property rights for elites and insecurity for everyone else. It can have markets that work well for insiders and badly for outsiders.

Acemoglu and Robinson call attention to this political foundation. Inclusive economic institutions broaden opportunity: more people can invest, learn, migrate, build, and challenge incumbents. Extractive institutions concentrate power and rewards: elites can block change that threatens them, even when change would raise total prosperity.

Liberty matters for progress when it gives many people real permission to use their knowledge, invest in the future, and challenge incumbents.

Can a society have strong property rights and still be extractive? Whose property rights would be protected, and whose would be weak?

Why are property rights important for progress?

  1. They remove all uncertainty from economic life.
  2. They give people confidence to invest, trade, and experiment because the returns are more secure.
  3. They make politics irrelevant to markets.
  4. They guarantee that everyone benefits equally from growth.

The likely student mistake is to treat “property rights” as morally self-evident rather than institutionally designed. Ask: property rights for whom, over what, enforced by whom, against whom? This is where the Acemoglu/Robinson reading complicates a simple Hayekian frame.

Inclusive institutions and creative destruction

Progress threatens existing power.

New firms threaten old firms. New technologies threaten old jobs. New voters threaten old elites. New ideas threaten old certainties. This is why Acemoglu and Robinson emphasize political institutions. Economic growth requires creative destruction, but incumbents often have the political power to stop it.

Inclusive institutions do not eliminate conflict. They create rules that make conflict productive rather than permanently exclusionary: losers can organize, appeal, compete, and try again. Extractive institutions let those with power block entry, seize gains, or make rules that preserve their position.

This connects Hayek to Acemoglu and Robinson. Hayek explains why decentralized knowledge matters. Acemoglu and Robinson explain why decentralization is not enough unless political institutions let people act on that knowledge.

Progress requires both information and permission: people must know what opportunities exist, and institutions must let them pursue those opportunities.

If a new technology would raise total income but destroy the income of a politically powerful group, what kinds of institutions make adoption more likely? What kinds make blockage more likely?

How do Acemoglu and Robinson complicate a simple market story?

  1. They claim markets never support growth.
  2. They show that political institutions determine who gets to use markets and on what terms.
  3. They reduce prosperity to geography alone.
  4. They argue that technology has no role in prosperity.

This is a good setup for Yuen Yuen Ang if you keep her in Session 11. Acemoglu/Robinson give a clean inclusive/extractive distinction; Ang complicates it by asking how development can begin under messy, transitional, and partially corrupt institutions.

Connections

Builds on: Session 8 showed how regional institutions shape innovation. Session 10 scales up to the national level: prices, property rights, and political rules determine whether people can coordinate and challenge incumbents.

Sets up: Session 11 on escaping poverty traps will ask how poor societies build developmental capacity when the ideal institutions described here are incomplete, contested, or still emerging.

Arc note: This session gives students the institutional baseline before the course turns to poverty traps and inequality. If they leave with only “markets good” or “institutions good,” the session has failed. The target understanding is subtler: decentralized progress needs price signals, secure rights, and political institutions broad enough to let many people act.

Which pairing best captures the relationship between Hayek and Acemoglu/Robinson in this session?

  1. Hayek explains decentralized knowledge; Acemoglu and Robinson explain the political institutions that make participation possible.
  2. Hayek explains extractive institutions; Acemoglu and Robinson explain price theory.
  3. Both readings make exactly the same argument in different words.
  4. Both readings argue that history does not matter.

Reading guide

Required

Hayek, F. A. “The Use of Knowledge in Society.” American Economic Review 35, no. 4 (1945): 519-530. Free at Econlib.

What to look for: Do not read Hayek as a generic defense of markets. Read him as making a knowledge argument. What kinds of information are hard to centralize? How do prices help people respond to facts they do not personally know?

Key argument: Prices are a coordination mechanism for dispersed knowledge; they allow millions of people to adjust to changing conditions without anyone needing to know the whole economy.

Bring to class: One example where a price communicates useful information, and one example where price signals are incomplete or misleading.

Acemoglu, Daron, and James A. Robinson. Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty. New York: Crown, 2012. Introduction.

What to look for: Focus on the mechanism, not the slogan. What do inclusive institutions let ordinary people do? What do extractive institutions let elites prevent? Why does the same border town make the institutional argument so vivid?

Key argument: Prosperity depends on inclusive political and economic institutions that protect broad participation and allow creative destruction; poverty persists where extractive institutions concentrate power and block change.

Bring to class: One claim from the introduction that feels persuasive, and one case where you suspect the inclusive/extractive distinction may be too simple.

Recommended

McCloskey, Deirdre Nansen. “The Formula for a Richer World? Equality, Liberty, Justice.” Journal of Economic Perspectives 33, no. 3 (2019): 3-24.

A useful complement if you want a broader moral and historical defense of liberty as a condition for innovation, rather than a purely institutional account.

Teaching note on readings: Hayek is short but dense; Acemoglu and Robinson are vivid but sweeping. Pair them by asking students to distinguish three things: knowledge, incentives, and power. Prices help with knowledge; property rights help with incentives; inclusive institutions constrain power.
