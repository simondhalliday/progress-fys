Elections, Democracy, and Progress

Session 17 · Tuesday, November 3, 2026

How does expanding who can vote change what governments do—and what counts as progress?
NoteBefore class

Read the 15-page reading packet on Canvas. The packet is also attached to the weekly announcement. Bring your readings and annotations. We will use Our World in Data graphs in class; no advance preparation of those graphs is required.

A parent gains a political voice

Imagine a mother who wants her town to do more to keep children healthy. She can organize with neighbors and ask officials to act, but she cannot vote for the people who decide how public money is spent.

Now imagine that the voting rules change. Her family’s needs are the same, but elected officials have a new reason to listen. Whether that new voice changes policy is an empirical question.

CORE’s case study of women’s suffrage asks how gaining the vote affected public spending and children’s survival. Its race insight asks how the Civil Rights Movement expanded political participation and opportunity—and why formal rights have not eliminated unequal political influence.

How does expanding who can vote change what governments do? And is having a political voice itself a form of progress?

Democracy means more than an election

CORE distinguishes inclusive, fair, and decisive elections from the other institutions that make political participation meaningful: civil liberties and the rule of law. The ability to criticize officials, organize with others, and obtain information matters alongside the right to cast a ballot.

Having a vote and having an effective political voice are related, but they are not identical.

A country holds regular elections, but opposition newspapers cannot publish and some citizens face serious obstacles to voting. Which parts of CORE’s definition of democracy are missing?

Freedom as progress and a means to progress

Sen asks us to distinguish the value of participating in political life from the benefits participation can help produce. Political voice can matter in itself; it can also make governments more responsive to people’s needs. Public discussion helps people identify and debate those needs in the first place.

Political freedom can be both something people value and a way to secure other improvements in their lives.

If two societies achieved similar improvements in income and health, but only one allowed meaningful public debate and political participation, would they have made the same progress? Explain your answer using Sen.

Check your understanding

  1. Explain the difference between a legal right to vote, actual participation, and influence over policy.
  2. In the women’s suffrage case study, trace the proposed sequence from voting rights to government decisions to children’s health. What evidence would help distinguish causation from coincidence?
  3. Identify one gain associated with the Civil Rights Movement and one continuing barrier discussed in the race insight. How can both be true?

Reading guide

The packet contains all of Tuesday’s required selections, in this order:

Packet pages Reading What to look for
1–3 CORE, “Democracy as a political institution”, The Economy 2.0: Macroeconomics, Section 10.3. Main text and definitions; the Arrow biographical box and exercises are omitted. The rules that make political participation meaningful.
4–9 Amartya Sen, “Democracy as a Universal Value” (1999): “Democracy and Economic Development” and “The Functions of Democracy,” with notes. Freedom’s value in itself, its role in accountability, and the importance of public discussion.
10–11 CORE, “Women’s suffrage and the reduction in child mortality in the US”, The Economy 1.0, Section 22.10. How a change in voting rights could change spending and health outcomes.
12–15 Eric Bottorff, Trevon Logan, and Suresh Naidu, Persistent Racial Inequality in the United States: Section 3’s Civil Rights Movement subsection, including Figure 4 and the rest of the section; Section 6’s opening two paragraphs and “Political inequality today”, stopping before Exercise 7. Gains in rights and wellbeing alongside the barriers that remain.

Read historical statistics in their source context: Sen’s essay was published in 1999 and the CORE race insight in 2022. Bring one passage that helps you distinguish political progress from economic progress, and one question about the connection between them.
