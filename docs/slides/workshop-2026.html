The Course Format

A First-Year Seminar at Hopkins

  • Small group, high stakes discussion — roughly 12 students meeting weekly for two hours; designed for genuine intellectual encounter, not content delivery
  • Faculty as interlocutor, not lecturer — the seminar model asks students to have read before we meet, so that class time is entirely discussion
  • Interdisciplinary by design — economics, history, public health, philosophy, and literature all have a seat at the table; no single discipline owns the question
  • A research-university version of liberal arts — Hopkins does not have a college; the FYS is how we give first-year students something close to the seminar experience anyway
03 / 12
Pedagogical Choices

Light Reading, Rich Discussion

  • De-prioritize coverage — no textbook, no chapter-per-week march through a field; instead: shorter, curated readings that reward careful attention
  • Preparation for shared inquiry — students submit a reading question before each session; they arrive with something to argue, not just notes to copy
  • Participation and writing as the central assessments — seminar essays, reflection pieces, a final argument; the grade follows the discussion, not the exam
  • Broad range of text types — data visualisations and charts alongside history, alongside philosophy; students practice reading across modes, not just genres

The Hopkins FYS framework explicitly frames this as "reading for shared inquiry" — preparation that generates discussion rather than summarisation

The Other Half of the Title

(And How We Could Lose It)

  • The course title's parenthetical is not rhetorical decoration — it is the intellectual problem: progress is real, and unevenly shared, and reversible
  • Between-country inequality has fallen sharply since 1990 — so some progress is genuinely widespread; this matters for how we teach the data
  • Within-country inequality has risen in many wealthy nations; race, class, and place determine who captures the gains from growth
  • Progress can reverse — democratic backsliding, pandemic disruption, climate risk, opioid crises; the course takes these seriously rather than treating them as footnotes

Students who arrive skeptical that progress is "on balance a good thing" are not wrong to be skeptical — they are asking the right question one level deeper

Place as Pedagogy

Why Baltimore Matters

  • Students arrive in Baltimore before they attend any class — the city is their first empirical encounter with the course's central question
  • Abstract aggregate data stays abstract without a place to anchor it; Baltimore forces concreteness: whose life expectancy? which neighbourhood's child mortality?
  • Hopkins cannot be separated from Baltimore's story — the university's wealth and the city's disinvestment share a history; teaching progress here requires acknowledging that
  • The distribution question is not theoretical in Baltimore; within a few miles, median household incomes differ by a factor of seven or eight

Crack epidemic, homicide rates among the highest in the US; disinvestment in public housing and schools; the story of "The Wire," however stylised

Inner Harbour renewal, Station North arts district, biotech corridor; alongside persistent poverty, the 2015 uprising, and homicide rates that remain high
The
question
Is this a story of progress interrupted, or a story of whose progress counted? The course asks students to hold both possibilities.

What the Course Doesn't Resolve

The Hardest Things to Teach

  • Students who arrive skeptical — often for historically well-grounded reasons — and the risk of either dismissing that scepticism or letting it collapse the course's argument entirely
  • "Isn't Baltimore proof that progress fails?" — this is a good-faith question that deserves a good-faith answer, not a pivot to the global aggregate; the city is in the room
  • Teaching regress without abandoning the frame — the opioid crisis, democratic backsliding, and climate risk are not footnotes; how do you hold them and the hockey sticks at the same time?
  • What a twelve-student seminar can and cannot do — the discussion gets deep; the coverage does not; the course is a bet that depth matters more than breadth for first-year students
